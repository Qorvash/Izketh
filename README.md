# Izketh

> The desert pays its rain to a sovereign made of thunderless bone.

## The Tribute of the Western Dunes

Every ninth year, the western dunes send an envoy to the city. No one sees the envoy arrive. At dawn, a shallow depression appears in the governor's courtyard, precisely the length and breadth of a kneeling person. Within it rests a ceramic jar sealed with yellow wool.

The jar contains rain that has not fallen. Its weight is entered in the city's accounts as an absence. The officials responsible for the weighing wear sandals of different sizes, so that no two footprints can accidentally describe the envoy's return.

The ceremony ends when the wool becomes damp. A clerk then carries the jar to the northern gate and leaves it beneath the statue of an animal whose head was removed before the city received its name.

By sunset, the jar is empty. The depression in the courtyard remains for another year.

## The Clerk Who Recorded a Weight

In the year of the green locust, a clerk entered a number where the account required an absence. The number was small. Its effect was not observed until the following morning, when every well in the city began returning sand.

The sand was cold and smelled of cedar. In each bucket lay a little fragment of ceramic bearing the same number. The clerk tried striking it from his page. Beneath the ink appeared a second number, written in a hand resembling his own but lacking the final stroke of every letter.

He was sent beyond the northern gate with the empty jar and a loaf of unleavened bread. Before departing, he noticed that the headless statue had acquired a shadow with seven horns.

The shadow pointed toward the western dunes, though the sun stood behind them.

## The Caravan of Unburdened Camels

On the third day, the clerk met a caravan whose camels carried no visible loads. Their handlers walked behind them with their eyes covered by strips of blue linen. Each animal left five footprints instead of four.

The caravan master offered the clerk a cup of warm dust. Through its rising vapor could be seen a city with its towers beneath the ground and its wells open toward the sky.

He asked what the clerk carried. The clerk showed the jar. The caravan master examined its seal, then placed one grain of cedar-scented sand upon the rim.

The jar filled with a sound like rain passing through dry leaves. The camels knelt together. Across their empty backs appeared the outline of a long white spine, stretching beyond the caravan in both directions.

The clerk did not drink the dust. The caravan master accepted this as an answer.

## The Palace Beneath the Pressure

They reached a place where the dunes had stopped moving. Beneath the sand lay a palace whose walls were held upright by the weight above them. Removing even a handful would have caused its corridors to close.

The caravan master pressed the empty jar against the ground. A passage opened sideways through the dune. At its far end lay the sovereign: an enormous dragon assembled from pale bones, its joints filled with darkness instead of flesh.

Its seven horns rested against the palace ceiling. Within its rib cage hung hundreds of ceramic jars. Each contained a different weather, though none moved or shone. The clerk recognized his city's rain by the sound of leaves.

Before the sovereign stood a table bearing the city's accounts. His incorrect number occupied an entire page. Around it, the paper had become thin enough to reveal a dark well beneath the table.

## The Figure That Could Not Be Erased

Along the table's edge were four groups of marks:

*Iz — keth — om — rau.*

The clerk read them without assigning them a value. His jar became heavy. The figure on the page began to turn, exposing another side that resembled the courtyard's kneeling depression.

He understood that his number had not described the rain. It had offered the city a body in which to receive it. The wells were returning the material from which that body would be made.

He broke his bread into seven pieces and placed them around the page. The bone sovereign lowered one horn. Its shadow entered the incorrect figure and removed a shape too narrow to be a person.

The page became blank. In the jar, the sound of leaves ceased.

## The Account Filed Afterward

The clerk returned before the ninth day, although no caravan accompanied him. The jar contained a single blue sandal. Its sole bore no mark, but inside it lay a little cedar-colored footprint.

The wells gave water again. The courtyard's depression vanished. At the northern gate, the headless animal cast an ordinary shadow, except during rain, when seven pale horns could be seen against the wall.

The clerk was forbidden to weigh anything thereafter. He became responsible for the blank spaces between accounts, inspecting each by candlelight and leaving his signature only in the margin.

The dunes still send their envoy every ninth year. The officials still wear mismatched sandals. The yellow wool still becomes damp before sunset.

Yet beneath the unmoving sand, one jar hangs empty inside the sovereign's ribs, and a number too small to be written walks slowly around it, looking for a place to kneel.
